Wildlife rescue and rehabilitation in Malaysia

A second chance
for Malaysia’s wildlife

Every year, members of the public and other agencies hand thousands of wild animals over to Malaysia’s wildlife department. What happens next? Follow public records from the people who take them in, nurse them back to health and return them to the forest.

01 · Into care · Which animals are handed in, and where?

Thousands of animals are handed in every year

When someone finds a wild animal in trouble, or another agency confiscates one, it can be handed over to PERHILITAN, the Department of Wildlife and National Parks. Its annual reports count these handovers for each state in Peninsular Malaysia.

Reptiles far outnumber mammals and birds

Reptiles made up 14,146 of the animals handed over in 2024. Mammals made up 1,683 and birds just 205.

A handover is a record of contact between people and wildlife. It does not count how many animals live in the wild.

02 · Recovery · What happens inside care?

Recovery takes time, and not every story ends well

Rehabilitation means preparing an animal to survive on its own again. The national rescue centre’s sun-bear guidelines describe a quarantine period on arrival, regular health and behaviour checks, and release only for animals that are healthy and behave like wild animals. International guidance adds that released animals should not depend on people for food.

A closer look · Sun bears in Sabah

Nine bears, five years of records

The Bornean Sun Bear Conservation Centre (BSBCC) in Sepilok cares for bears that were orphaned, kept as pets or confiscated. Bears that are ready are taken to Tabin Wildlife Reserve.

Some are released straight into the forest (a hard release). Others, like Tenom and Pitang, first spend months on supervised forest walks.

Sources: BSBCC Annual Reports 2021–2025; Brunton et al. 2025 for Sunbearo’s and Loki’s outcomes; background: NWRC sun-bear guideline (2022) and IUCN (2025). No animal tracking data is used.

03 · Return · Where do animals go back to the wild?

Back to the forest

In 2024 the National Wildlife Rescue Centre returned 32 animals of 16 species to six protected areas. The report lists the release area for each species, so we can see which animals went where.

Reading the map

Penang National Park received the most

13 animals went to Penang National Park: seven birds of prey and owls, and six small mammals (civets, otters and a leopard cat).

The big cats and sun bears went to inland forests: leopards to Temenggor Forest Reserve, and clouded leopards and sun bears to Taman Negara in Terengganu.

04 · People · Who gives their time?

Care also takes people’s time

At BSBCC, volunteers prepare food, feed bears, build enrichment toys and clean enclosures. University interns also run school talks. The centre lists every volunteer placement in its annual report.

What we learned

Care is a chain of decisions, places and people.

  1. More animals are being handed in. Despite a small dip in 2022, handovers rose by about 40% between 2021 and 2024, and nine in ten are reptiles (charts 01 and 04).
  2. The mix differs from state to state. Most of Selangor’s handovers are mammals, while in Kelantan, Johor and Terengganu almost all are reptiles (chart 03).
  3. Rescue centres work in sudden waves. Two months in 2024 brought in more than 10,000 reptiles (chart 05).
  4. Release is a beginning, not a guaranteed happy ending. Three of the five sun bears released since 2021 later died (chart 06), and none of the 32 NWRC releases are followed up in the report (charts 07 and 08).
  5. People make the work possible. BSBCC’s volunteers rose from 7 in 2021 to 57 in 2025, most staying about two weeks (charts 09 to 12).

What these records cannot tell us

  • Why handovers rose: more animals in trouble, more people reporting, or better record-keeping.
  • How many released animals survive in the long term.
  • Anything about Sabah and Sarawak’s state agencies, which publish separately.

How to read these figures
  • Handovers are records of contact, not estimates of how many animals live in the wild or how well a state protects wildlife.
  • Handovers, rescue-centre intake, releases and the BSBCC bears are separate records. Dividing one by another does not give a success rate.
  • The bear timeline shows cases described in public reports, not every bear at BSBCC.
  • Volunteer placement days are calendar days on site, not hours worked, and do not measure the effect on animals.
  • Extraction notes and page references for every figure are in the data provenance record.

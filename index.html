Public records, 2021–2025

Wildlife rescue and rehabilitation in Malaysia

Every year, members of the public and other agencies hand thousands of wild animals over to Malaysia’s wildlife department. Some are found in trouble and others are confiscated. This page uses public reports to follow what happens to them: who takes them in, how they are cared for, and where they are released.

01 · Into care · Which animals are handed over, and where?

Wildlife handed over to PERHILITAN

When someone finds a wild animal in trouble, or another agency confiscates one, it can be handed over to PERHILITAN, the Department of Wildlife and National Parks. PERHILITAN’s annual reports count these handovers for every state in Peninsular Malaysia. In 2024 there were 16,034.

About one in ten was a mammal, and about one in a hundred was a bird.

A handover records contact between people and wildlife. It does not show how many animals live in the wild.

Both maps cover Peninsular Malaysia only, because Sabah and Sarawak keep separate records. PERHILITAN reports the Federal Territories as one figure (1,038 animals), so the maps treat Kuala Lumpur, Putrajaya and Labuan as one unit, drawn at Kuala Lumpur, and its rate per km² uses the three territories’ combined area.

Sources: PERHILITAN Annual Reports 2024 (Table 34), 2023 (Table 46), 2022 (national total, p105) and 2021 (Table 20). Boundaries: geoBoundaries / OpenStreetMap.

02 · Recovery · What happens in care?

Intake and rehabilitation

Rehabilitation prepares an animal to survive on its own again. The national rescue centre’s sun-bear guidelines describe a quarantine period on arrival, regular health and behaviour checks, and release only for animals that are healthy and behave like wild animals. International guidance adds that released animals should not depend on people for food. At the National Wildlife Rescue Centre in Sungkai, the number of animals arriving changes sharply from month to month.

Source: PERHILITAN 2024, numeric table under Figure 26 (p107); 2023, Table 31 (p89) and text. Where Figure 26’s bar labels differ from its table, the table is used.

A closer look · Sun bears in Sabah

Sun bears at BSBCC, Sabah

The Bornean Sun Bear Conservation Centre (BSBCC) in Sepilok cares for bears that were orphaned, kept as pets or confiscated. Bears that are ready are taken to Tabin Wildlife Reserve.

Some bears are released directly into the forest, which BSBCC calls a hard release. Others, such as Tenom and Pitang, first spend months on supervised forest walks, training for a soft release. BSBCC’s reports from 2021 to 2025 describe nine of these bears.

Sources: BSBCC Annual Reports 2021–2025; Brunton et al. 2025 for Sunbearo’s and Loki’s outcomes; background: NWRC sun-bear guideline (2022) and IUCN (2025). No animal tracking data is used.

03 · Return · Where do animals go back to the wild?

Release destinations

In 2024 the National Wildlife Rescue Centre released 32 animals of 16 species into six protected areas. Its report names the release area for each species.

Reading the map

Where each group went

Thirteen animals went to Penang National Park: seven birds of prey and owls, and six small mammals (civets, otters and a leopard cat).

The big cats and sun bears went to inland forests. The leopards went to Temenggor Forest Reserve, and the clouded leopards and sun bears to Taman Negara in Terengganu.

04 · People · Who gives their time?

Volunteers and interns

At BSBCC, volunteers prepare food, feed the bears, build enrichment toys and clean enclosures, and university interns run school talks. The centre’s annual report lists every volunteer placement with its dates and the volunteer’s country.

Sources: BSBCC Annual Reports 2021–2025; 2025 placements from Appendix I (pp30–32). Placement length = end date − start date + 1. Names are not shown.

Summary

What these records show

  1. Handovers rose by about 40% between 2021 and 2024, despite a small dip in 2022, and nearly nine in ten were reptiles (charts 01 and 04).
  2. For their land area, small states such as Pulau Pinang handed over the most animals. Most of Selangor’s handovers were mammals, while in Kelantan, Johor and Terengganu almost all were reptiles (charts 02 and 03).
  3. Reptiles reached the national rescue centre in large waves: two months in 2024 brought in more than 10,000. Mammals arrived through the year (chart 05).
  4. Three of the five featured sun bears released since 2021 later died (chart 06). The NWRC report does not follow up on any of its 32 releases (charts 07 and 08).
  5. BSBCC’s volunteers rose from 7 in 2021 to 57 in 2025, and most placements lasted two weeks or less (charts 09 to 12).

What these records cannot tell us

  • Why handovers rose: more animals in trouble, more people reporting, or better record-keeping.
  • How many released animals survive in the long term.
  • Anything about Sabah and Sarawak’s state agencies, which publish separately.

How to read these figures
  • Handovers are records of contact, not estimates of how many animals live in the wild or how well a state protects wildlife.
  • Handovers, rescue-centre intake, releases and the BSBCC bears are separate records. Dividing one by another does not give a success rate.
  • The bear tree shows cases described in public reports, not every bear at BSBCC.
  • Volunteer placement days are calendar days on site, not hours worked, and do not measure the effect on animals.
  • Extraction notes and page references for every figure are in the data provenance record.
